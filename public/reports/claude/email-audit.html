01

Where the value actually sits

Automated flows and one-off campaigns are not close to equal, and the account is investing almost entirely in the weaker one.

Over the year, flows sent 193,681 emails and returned $242,875. Campaigns sent 1,597,645 emails — 8× the volume — and returned $105,838. Every flow email is worth 19× more than every campaign email.

This is normal in direction but extreme in degree. The problem is not that campaigns underperform flows; it's that campaigns are absorbing nearly nine-tenths of the sending — and with it, nearly all of the unsubscribe and spam-complaint cost that is destroying the list.

02

The list is being consumed faster than it is built

This is the finding that governs everything else. An email program is an asset that compounds — this one is depreciating.

9,188 people opted in. 15,127 opted out. That is 1.65 people leaving for every one who joins, and a net loss of 5,939 subscribers over the year. Every month of sending at the current rate makes next year's email revenue smaller.

The relationship to send volume is direct and visible in the data. October 2025 — 13 campaigns, 170,205 emails — cost 3,138 unsubscribes. June 2026, when no campaigns went out at all, cost 164. The two months that ran the lightest schedules, May and June 2026, are the only two months in the window where the list grew.

Behind the flow, the composition is worse than the totals suggest. 23,245 profiles have not engaged in 365 days — against roughly 37,000 mailable in total, that is close to two-thirds of the list already dead. The Engaged 180 Days segment stands at 3,571 and has lost 3,017 members in the past 30 days alone.

03

Findings

Critical

Spam complaints are within range of Gmail's enforcement threshold

Flows are averaging a 0.107% spam-complaint rate — more than five times the 0.02% that mailbox providers treat as healthy. Gmail begins throttling and bulk-foldering at 0.30%, and four individual flows are already sitting near it. If deliverability degrades, every number in this report falls at once, including the flows that are working.

Critical

Six campaigns shipped with no working link

Six sends over 2,000 recipients recorded literally zero clicks against normal open rates — the signature of a broken or missing call to action, not weak copy. That is 84,416 emails that could not convert by construction.

"October Campaign 3 — Something Big Is Coming" reached 15,395 people, earned zero clicks and zero revenue, and cost 502 unsubscribes at a 3.26% rate. There is no QA gate catching this.

High

People open, then don't click

Campaigns average a 55.1% open rate and a 1.09% click rate — a click-to-open of just 2.0%. Subject lines are doing their job; the emails behind them are not. (Treat the open rate as directional — Apple Mail Privacy Protection inflates it.)

The cost lands as churn rather than indifference: 0.75% of recipients unsubscribe per campaign, roughly 10× a healthy rate. Across ~43 campaigns a year, that arithmetic alone accounts for the list decline. Twelve of 143 campaigns produced no revenue at all.

High

Four cart-recovery ideas have been forked into 24 live flows

Abandoned Cart exists as 6 separate flows, Browse Abandonment as 8, Abandoned Checkout as 7, Site Abandonment as 4 — variants suffixed [Retention], Triple Pixel, Triple Whale and Black Friday, many running simultaneously against overlapping triggers.

The effect is over-messaging the same person from parallel flows, split data that makes none of the variants conclusive, and seasonal flows still live in September. Abandoned Cart 2.0 Triple Whale earned $755 all year from 710 sends while contributing to the same fatigue as the flows that earn real money.

High

The welcome series stops working after email one

Welcome flows are the single best asset in the account — $133,117, or 55% of all flow revenue. But the value is concentrated almost entirely in the first message, and the rest of the sequence is close to dead weight.

On the Top Bar series, email one returns $27.68 per recipient and carries 75% of the flow's revenue; emails two through eight return $0.86–$2.93, and one of them returns nothing at all on 618 sends. Meanwhile welcome email one is itself running a 2.4% unsubscribe rate — the highest-value message in the account is also among its most costly.

Opportunity

The best-converting flow gets the least traffic

Abandoned Checkout 2.0 earns $4.81 per recipient — it caught someone mid-purchase, and it converts. It reached 4,620 people all year. Site Abandonment 2.0 earns $1.40 and reached 16,217 — 3.5× the volume for the same total revenue.

Volume is flowing to the weakest intent signal. The checkout-abandon trigger is either under-firing or under-covered, and closing that gap is the cheapest revenue in this report.

Opportunity

Flows that should exist, don't — and one that does, doesn't work

There is no Back in Stock flow. The Subscribed to Back in Stock metric is live, a dedicated "American Rebounder Back in Stock Subscribers" list is collecting people, and no flow is triggered by it. Those are buyers who named the exact product they want and are waiting.

There is also no winback or lapsed-customer flow for the 23,245 unengaged profiles — they are going straight to sunset without an attempt to reactivate. VIP Flow & Referral has been live since 2023 and has produced $0 from 576 sends. SMS is effectively unbuilt: four recipients in the entire year.

What a single send is worth

Ranking every flow by revenue per recipient shows exactly where an extra email is worth sending — and where it is destroying value.

05

Recommendations

Ordered by return on effort. The first three are defensive — they stop losses that compound — and should run before anything designed to add volume.

1

Stop sending to people who aren't reading

The single highest-value change, and it costs nothing but restraint. Campaigns are going to audiences far wider than the engaged base, and paying for it in unsubscribes and spam complaints.

  • Cap all promotional campaigns to engaged-120-day as the default audience; require a deliberate exception to go wider.
  • Add a preference centre — "fewer emails" is an option 15,127 people were never offered before they chose "none."
  • Suppress the 23,245 unengaged from broadcast entirely, and route them to a winback sequence instead of a single 37,000-recipient sunset blast at a 0.25% complaint rate.
  • Set a hard frequency cap per profile per week across flows and campaigns combined.
Impact +$56K/yrEffort MediumAlso protects all other revenue
2

Put a QA gate in front of every send

Six broken campaigns and 84,416 wasted emails is a process failure, not a copy failure. This is a checklist, not a project.

  • Send a seed test to a real inbox and click every link before scheduling — no exceptions.
  • Add an automatic check that a campaign cannot schedule with zero tracked links.
  • Review the 12 zero-revenue campaigns for the same defect before reusing those templates.
Impact +$5.6K/yrEffort LowPrevents ~500 unsubs/incident
3

Consolidate 24 cart-recovery flows into 4

One canonical flow per intent — cart, checkout, browse, site — each with a single tested variant. Archive the Triple Pixel, Triple Whale and seasonal duplicates.

  • Keep the best-performing variant per family; archive the rest rather than pausing them.
  • Turn off Black Friday flows still live in September, and schedule them as seasonal activations instead.
  • Consolidating also fixes the split-data problem: one flow per intent finally produces a readable test.
Impact Reduces fatigueEffort LowUnblocks real testing
4

Rebuild welcome emails two through eight

Email one proves the audience converts. The rest of the sequence isn't earning its place — and it's where the 2.4% unsubscribe rate is being paid.

  • Cut both welcome series to 4–5 messages and rewrite the survivors around objections rather than repeated discounts: sizing and space, joint impact versus running, the 30-day trial, assembly and warranty.
  • Consolidate the nine welcome variants into one series with a single offer test running at a time.
  • Move the strongest offer out of email one alone and give email three a distinct reason to act.
Impact +$25–35K/yrEffort MediumBase $133K flow
5

Build the three flows that are missing

Each targets people who have already signalled intent, which is why flow economics apply rather than campaign economics.

  • Back in Stock — the list and the trigger metric already exist; only the flow is missing. Highest intent in the account.
  • Winback for lapsed customers, ahead of sunset — a rebounder is a considered purchase with real accessory and replacement follow-on.
  • Post-purchase cross-sell — Order Delivered reaches 21,495 people at $0.20 each. That is a large, warm, well-timed audience being used only for logistics.
  • Either rebuild or archive VIP Flow & Referral; at $0 across three years it is currently just fatigue.
Impact +$15–25K/yrEffort Medium
6

Fix checkout-abandon coverage, then fix campaign clicks

Two separate jobs, both about converting attention that already exists.

  • Audit why Abandoned Checkout reached only 4,620 people while Site Abandonment reached 16,217 — check trigger conditions, exclusion filters and flow-priority conflicts across the duplicate flows.
  • On campaigns, a 2.0% click-to-open against a 55% open rate is a body-content problem: one clear offer per email, one primary button above the fold, and real product imagery instead of discount-only layouts.
  • Test on the engaged segment only, so a losing test costs clicks rather than subscribers.

Method and caveats

How to read these numbers

  • Attribution is Klaviyo's, measured against the Shopify Placed Order metric on a last-touch basis. Email's true incremental contribution is lower than $348,713 — some of those buyers would have purchased anyway. The figure is right for comparing flows against campaigns, which is what it's used for here.
  • Open rates are inflated by Apple Mail Privacy Protection and should be read as directional only. The 55% campaign open rate against a 1.09% click rate is partly an MPP artefact — which is exactly why click-to-open, not open rate, is the metric used in the findings.
  • Flow and campaign reports are send-time based; the store-revenue and subscriber figures come from event-time aggregates. The two methods put email-attributed revenue at $348,713 and $349,221 respectively — a 0.15% difference, well within tolerance.
  • Both edge months are partial (8–30 Sep 2025 and 1–8 Sep 2026), so the first and last rows of the monthly table are not comparable to full months.
  • The ~37,000 mailable figure is inferred from the reach of Sunset Flow #2, which sent to 37,006 profiles in one message. Segment counts are current as of 8 Sep 2026, not period averages.
  • Opportunity figures are models, not forecasts. Each holds current conversion rates constant and varies one input; the basis column states the assumption in each case.
